The assignment comes first: a commercial network of twelve companies, animated over 60 days, with two static overviews beside it so distant days can still be compared. The in-lab exercises (180 days of weather as a multi-series line chart with a metric switch, range filtering and an animated marker) follow in Section 6.

1. Dataset

Companies — lab7_assignment_companies.csv
12 rows, one node each: id, company_name, sector (7 sectors) and region (Asia 4, Europe 4, North America 4).
Transactions — lab7_assignment_transactions_60days.csv
367 rows over days 1–60 (2026-01-01 to 2026-03-01), each a day's commercial activity between two companies: amount_usd ($7,330–$33,892), transaction_type (goods, shipping, components, materials, services) and transaction_count (1–5). Source and target order varies between rows, so the links are read as undirected: the 367 rows collapse to 27 distinct relationships, 3 to 10 of which are active on any one day.
Rhythm
Every relationship recurs on a strict 2-, 3-, 4- or 5-day cycle, so a pair being absent from one day usually means "not its turn", not "over". What the picture treats as an event is therefore a relationship's first and last day across the whole period, not its day-to-day gaps.
Caveat
The companies and transactions are fabricated for visualization practice. Nothing here describes a real firm or a real trade.

2. Animated temporal network

One frame is one day. Press Play, or drag the slider to inspect a single day; every other control below the transport row re-renders the same frame. Nodes never move and never leave — a company with no trade that day greys out and shrinks to its minimum, so the reader tracks the same twelve positions for all 60 days. Hover any node or link for its exact values, click a link to pin it (it also lights up in the grid in Section 4), and drag a node if a label is covered. The three dashed columns are regions, so a link that crosses a column is a cross-regional relationship.

3. The whole period at once

Animation maps data time onto animation time, which means the viewer has to remember earlier frames to compare them. These two strips keep the shape of all 60 days on screen while one day is animated. Click or drag anywhere on them to send the animation to that day.

Both strips share the day axis; each is its own chart with its own vertical scale, because relationship counts and dollars do not belong on one axis. The dark bar and the caret above it are the current day; with a trailing window selected, the lighter blue bars are the other days in the frame.

4. Which relationship existed when

The one thing the animation cannot hold still is whether a relationship exists at all. All 27 are drawn here as rows, ordered by the day they first appear, so onset, rhythm and close-out read off a single picture. Click any cell to jump the animation to that day.

A filled cell means the two companies traded that day; darker means a larger amount. The two dots on the left are the companies' regions — two unlike colours is a cross-regional relationship — and the short line is the dash pattern that identifies this relationship's transaction type in the network above. The grey band spans each relationship's first to last day, and the black outline is the current frame.

5. Encodings and why they were chosen

Region is the only categorical colour in the network: blue for Asia, orange for Europe, aqua for North America — the palette's first three slots, which validate all-pairs (worst colour-blind ΔE 9.2, worst normal-vision ΔE 24.0). Aqua measures 2.74:1 against the page, below 3:1, so the relief rule applies and is honoured twice over: every node prints its company name, and Section 4 ships a table view. Region is also horizontal position, through a weak forceX per region, which is what makes a cross-regional relationship something you see as a line crossing a column rather than something you work out from two hues.

Sector is shape (7 d3.symbol types) and the day's traded volume is symbol area, so a company can grow and shrink over the 60 days without changing identity. Area, not radius, is proportional to the dollar value, and idle companies stop at a fixed floor rather than vanishing. A company that opens a new relationship gets a dark ring for that frame.

On the links, amount is stroke width and transaction type is dash pattern — type is deliberately not a second colour scale, because five more hues competing with three region hues in the same picture is exactly how a node-link diagram becomes unreadable. The temporal events sit on a third channel again, a glyph at the link's midpoint: a filled dot for the first day of a relationship (with the line drawn in ink and a brief pulse on that dot) and a hollow ring for its last. Day-to-day appearance and disappearance is carried by the D3 join itself, fading in on enter and out on exit over a duration tied to the playback speed.

The mental map is preserved by taking positions out of the animation entirely. The force simulation runs once, on the aggregate 60-day network — all 27 relationships, pulled together in proportion to how often they trade — with a collision radius set to the largest a node ever gets. Those positions are then pinned, so a growing node can never push its neighbours around and a company sits in the same place on day 3 and day 57. Because position is no longer available to show clustering, clusters are drawn explicitly: a soft blob behind each connected component, and a count in the summary row. Re-settle layout releases the pins and re-runs the simulation if a reader wants a different arrangement.

Trailing 3 days and trailing 7 days exist because the relationships recur on 2- and 3-day cycles: a single day shows only a slice of the structure that is actually in force. Three days is roughly the full current relationship set, and it is the view that makes the phase changes at days 21 and 41 obvious. One day is the default, as the assignment specifies.

6. The temporal-network questions

1. How does overall connectivity change across the 60 days?

daily strips links per day value per day

It rises, but a trend line would hide the more interesting half of the answer. Averaged over quarters of the period, active relationships go 5.4 → 5.7 → 6.3 → 7.1 per day and traded value goes $76k → $91k → $114k → $143k per day — value nearly doubles while link count grows by a third, so the later relationships are also individually larger.

On top of that trend sits a hard short cycle, which is what the upper strip actually shows: because every relationship recurs every 2 or 3 days, the days interleave into a repeating pattern. In the last 20 days it is exactly six-long — 5, 7, 9, 6, 6, 10 links — so day 51 carries $202k and day 52 carries $113k with nothing having changed structurally. Any claim about "a busy day" has to say where in that cycle the day falls.

2. Which companies become more central or more active?

node size relationship grid volume per day

Centrality is handed on rather than accumulated, and it happens in two steps that the grid dates precisely. Fusion Electronics (Technology, Asia) takes it first: on days 21 and 22 it opens three relationships at once — with Harbor Shipping, Lumen Distribution and Juniper Stores — and its volume jumps from $281k in days 1–15 to $623k in days 16–30. It ends the period with 8 of the 27 relationships, more than any other company, and is active on 53 of 60 days.

Then on days 41–42 the hub moves west. Cedar Retail opens three (Granite Wholesale, Keystone Materials, Harbor Shipping) and Ion Systems opens three (Delta Components, Apex Textiles, Fusion Electronics) while its dormant link to Juniper Stores revives. Cedar goes $135k → $305k → $666k across the last three quarters and Ion goes $134k → $333k → $838k, finishing as the single largest node by volume — while Fusion, having lost four partners on day 40, falls back to $427k. Evergreen Foods never joins in: two relationships, active on 27 of 60 days, $285k in total, and the smallest node in almost every frame.

3. Are there periods of relatively separate clusters?

cluster outlines clusters count

The network is never a single component on any of the 60 days — 2 to 4 separate clusters, every day. The fragmentation is worst on the sparse days of the 3-day rhythm: on days 1, 7 and 13 only three relationships are active and they are three disjoint pairs (Cedar with Evergreen, Ion with Lumen, Granite with Harbor), so the largest cluster is two companies and half the network is idle. Those days are also single-continent days: not one Asian company trades. The mirror case appears in the middle of the period — on days 24 and 36 a six-company Europe–Asia cluster is active and no North American company trades at all.

Connectivity peaks on the last phase's big days (42, 48, 54, 60), where the largest cluster reaches 8 of 12 companies — but even there it is still three clusters, because Granite with Keystone and Evergreen with Lumen sit off to the side on their own rhythm.

4. Which relationships appear, disappear or strengthen?

relationship grid link glyphs first / last day

Ten relationships span the whole period by first and last day — they start by day 3 and are still trading at day 58–60. They are the backbone, and they are the cheap ones. Around that backbone there are two clean turnover events. Day 20 closes three (BlueRiver with Delta, Cedar with Fusion, Harbor with Keystone) and sends Ion with Juniper dormant for exactly twenty days. Days 39–40 close six more: four of them are Fusion's (with Granite, Harbor, Lumen and Juniper), and the other two are Granite with Harbor and Delta with Keystone. Granite loses two of its three partners in that one step and its volume drops 24% in the final quarter, even though Cedar opens a new line to it on day 41.

Newer is bigger, consistently. The eight relationships that open on days 41–42 average $15k–$30k per trading day; Fusion with Ion averages $30,058 and Cedar with Keystone $28,230, against $8,874 for Cedar with Evergreen, which has been running since day 1. In the grid this shows as the lower rows being uniformly darker than the upper ones. The backbone itself does not thicken: of the nine that trade continuously, every one is within ±6% of its own first-half average in the second half. The single exception proves the pattern — Ion with Juniper is up 59%, and only because it did not run continuously: it came back after its twenty dark days at the new, higher level. Growth arrives as new links, not as thicker old ones.

5. Does the network become more cross-regional?

region columns region dots cross-regional share

Yes, steadily. Cross-regional links are 63% of the active links in days 1–15, then 65%, 67% and 72% in days 46–60 — and seven of the eight relationships that open on days 41–42 cross a region boundary. Watching the animation with the region columns on, the late frames are visibly more diagonal than the early ones.

The sharper version of the same shift is which regions are present at all. In the first 40 days, 10 days have a whole region sitting out — no Asian company trades on days 1, 7, 13 or 19; no North American company trades on days 24, 30 or 36. In the last 20 days that happens on none: all three regions trade on every single one. That is the real change, and it is a stronger statement than the percentage — not just more cross-border links, but no more days when a region is absent.

7. In-lab exercises (Tasks 1–11)

180 daily observations for eight cities from lab7_historical_weather.csv, parsed with d3.timeParse("%Y-%m-%d") (the parsed rows are also logged to the browser console, per Task 2). A synthetic historical-style dataset: plausible seasonal shape, not meteorology.

Range

Hover the plot for a crosshair that snaps to a date and reads out every selected city at once — the pointer never has to land on a 2 px line. Drag across the plot to zoom into a date range (d3.brushX, with xScale.invert turning the pixels back into dates), or type one into the date fields. Play walks a marker along every visible line; the slider scrubs it, and both are limited to the range currently shown. Cities are toggled from the legend, and each line is labelled at its right end so identity never rests on colour alone.

Static versus animated (Task 11). The line chart wins outright here, and the two forms in this lab show why. Time is mapped to space, so 180 days are on screen at once. Add Sydney and Toronto and the hemispheres swap in a single glance: Sydney opens January at 20°C and falls to 9.5°C by May while Toronto does the reverse, and the two lines cross in March. Switch the metric to precipitation and Cairo's line is flat on zero for all 180 days while Singapore's totals 919 mm. Any two dates can be compared by eye. The animated marker adds nothing to those questions — it re-tells information the line already holds, one frame at a time, and forces the viewer to remember what it just showed.

Animation earns its place in the assignment instead, because the thing changing there is not a value but a structure: links that exist on Tuesday and not on Wednesday have no natural place on a spatial axis. Even so, the network is not left to animation alone — the two day strips and the relationship grid put the whole period back on screen, which is the overview + animation  + time-controls combination rather than animation by itself.
