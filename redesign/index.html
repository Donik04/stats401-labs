Individual Project: Redesigning the World Happiness Rankings

Every March, the headline “Finland is the happiest country in the world” is written from one chart: Figure 2.1 of the World Happiness Report. This page takes that chart apart and rebuilds it in D3, using the report’s own published data.

1. The original visualization

Title

Full report (PDF) · Data for Figure 2.1 (xlsx) · Statistical Appendix (PDF) · WHR data sharing page
Data shown
147 countries and territories. For each one: the average answer to the Cantril ladder question (“imagine a ladder from 0, the worst possible life for you, to 10, the best possible life”) in the Gallup World Poll, pooled over 2023–2025 at roughly 1,000 respondents per country per year; a 95% confidence interval for that average; and a 95% range for the country’s rank. Each score is then split into seven stacked sub-bars: the part a regression attributes to each of six factors (log GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceptions of corruption), measured from Dystopia, an imaginary country with the world’s lowest value of every factor and a ladder score of 1.16, plus a final “Dystopia + residual” sub-bar for whatever is left.

The original, as printed across three pages of the report (screenshots rendered from the PDF by build_data.py). Click a page to open it at full size.

2. What the figure is for

Intended message

First, where every country stands: Finland is alone at the top with 7.76, Afghanistan is alone at the bottom with 1.45, and the gap between them is more than six points on a ten-point scale. Second, the ranking is uneven. The report’s own text points out that the top 20 countries sit within less than one point of each other while the bottom 20 are spread three times as widely, and that for several mid-table countries the 95% range of possible ranks is wider than 25 places. Third, six measurable things explain a large share of the differences: income, having someone to count on, health, freedom, generosity and trust. Whatever they do not explain is left over as a residual.

Audience

There are two groups of readers. The larger one is journalists, policymakers and the general public. The figure is reproduced in news coverage every year, and most of these readers come to it to find their own country and its neighbours. They have not read Box 2.3 or the Statistical Appendix. The smaller group is researchers and analysts who care about the decomposition. A good design has to work for the first group without misleading the second.

Tasks

  1. T1 · Look up a country’s rank and score.
  2. T2 · Compare two countries and judge whether the difference between them is real or within the uncertainty.
  3. T3 · See the distribution: where the countries bunch together and where they spread out.
  4. T4 · Compare one factor across countries: which countries get the most out of social support, and where does mine stand on health?
  5. T5 · Read one country’s profile: which factors raise its score and which hold it back.
  6. T6 · Find outliers: countries that are much happier, or much unhappier, than the six factors predict.
  7. T7 · Compare within a region, such as Central Asia or Western Europe.

3. Critique

Strengths

S1. A ranked list that doubles as a lookup table

Countries are sorted by score, and every row prints the rank, the 95% rank range and the exact score beside the name, for example “53 (43–65) Uzbekistan (6.283)”. The task most readers come with (T1) needs no decoding at all, and the numbers are precise rather than estimated from bar lengths.

S2. Honest length on a common zero baseline

Every bar starts at zero on a labelled 0–8 axis, so the total length is a faithful length encoding of the score for nearly every country. Length from a shared baseline is one of the most accurately judged visual channels, and nothing is exaggerated by a truncated axis. The 7.76 against 1.45 gap looks as large as it is.

S3. Uncertainty and explanation in the same chart

Few league tables show uncertainty at all. This one prints confidence intervals for both score and rank, and it keeps the six-factor explanation next to the ranking instead of in a separate chart. The factor colours and their order stay the same on all three pages, so a reader who learns the legend once can use it everywhere.

Weaknesses and opportunities

W1. Stacked sub-bars make the factors impossible to compare across countries

marks & channels comparisons T4 · T5
Problem
Only the first segment, GDP, sits on the common baseline. Every other segment starts wherever the one before it ended. To compare social support in Kazakhstan (rank 33) with Uzbekistan (rank 53), a reader has to compare two purple segments that begin at different x-positions, twenty rows apart.
Why it matters
The report says the sub-bars exist to explain why countries differ, but judging unaligned lengths is one of the least accurate visual judgements people make. In practice a reader can see the GDP segment and the total length, and very little else. T4 and T5 are not really possible with this design.
Improvement
Unstack the bar. Give each factor its own column with its own zero line, keep the same rows, and allow the table to be sorted by any column.

W2. The loudest segment is a constant plus an error term, and it can make the bar wrong

visual hierarchy colour T1 · T6
Problem
The last, bright cyan sub-bar is “Dystopia (1.16) + residual”: the same 1.16 for every country plus the model’s prediction error. It is the longest sub-bar for 38 of the 147 countries (Costa Rica and Mexico, for example), and it is drawn in the most saturated colour on the page. A stacked bar also cannot go below zero. When the residual is below −1.16, this segment disappears but the other six still stack, so the bar overshoots the country’s actual score. Hong Kong’s bars reach 5.87 although its score is 5.57 and its confidence interval ends at 5.65. Sri Lanka’s reach 4.24 against a score of 4.01.
Why it matters
The chart gives the most visual weight to the least meaningful quantity. The report’s text says that “the overall length of each country bar represents the average response”, but for these rows it does not. The rows that are drawn wrongly are the most interesting ones for T6: countries far unhappier than their circumstances predict.
Improvement
Take the constant out of the marks and state it once. Show the residual in its own column as a bar that can go either side of zero. Draw the score as a separate mark, so that no decomposition can misstate it.

W3. Every sub-bar is measured from an abstract and fragile zero point

scales & axes audience fit T4 · T5
Problem
“Explained by” is measured relative to Dystopia, which takes the lowest national value of each factor. For GDP, the lowest value belongs to Venezuela. The Statistical Appendix (Figure 24) lists Venezuela’s GDP per capita at $89, while the next lowest country, Yemen, is at $917. A value that low is very likely a data problem. Because of it, Venezuela’s GDP segment is exactly zero (its row begins with social support), its residual of +1.54 is the largest in the world, and the zero of every country’s GDP segment is set at $89.
Why it matters
Readers naturally take segment length to mean “how much income adds to happiness”. In fact the length depends on which country happens to be at the bottom, and nothing on the chart shows that. “Relative to a hypothetical Dystopia” is also a concept that the main audience has to look up before they can use it.
Improvement
Measure each factor from the average country instead: a bar to the right raises the predicted score above average, and a bar to the left lowers it. A difference from the mean does not depend on the minimum, so Venezuela’s anomaly then only affects Venezuela’s own bar. The report’s Dystopia view can stay one click away.

W4. Missing data looks the same as zero

labels honesty T5
Problem
Tajikistan (88) and the State of Palestine (109) have no bars at all, only a whisker, yet each is missing just one of the six factors (freedom and healthy life expectancy, respectively). The reason, that not every factor was available “even after the imputation process”, is only given in the Statistical Appendix, a separate 71-page document. Venezuela’s missing GDP segment is drawn exactly like a zero.
Why it matters
An empty row looks like either a score of zero or a printing error. When “no data” and “none” look identical, the reader cannot tell which one they are seeing, and five known values per country are thrown away.
Improvement
Draw every factor that exists, write “no data” in the cells that are empty, and flag doubtful values where they appear instead of in an appendix.

W5. Uncertainty and distribution are shown but not usable, and the list is cut into three pages

clutter & readability legends T2 · T3 · T7
Problem
The score intervals are whiskers a few pixels wide at the end of long bars, and the rank ranges are text in brackets. Two patterns the report’s own text emphasises are therefore invisible: the rank range widens to 44 places in the middle of the table (Jamaica, 30–73), and the top 20 fit inside 0.94 points while the bottom 20 spread over 2.86. The bars simply form a smooth ramp. The list is also split into pages of 48, 48 and 51 rows, each with its legend at the bottom, so countries on either side of a page break (48 and 49, 96 and 97) cannot be seen together, and there is no way to pull out a region. Seven hues have to be matched to a legend, and the generosity and corruption segments are often only 1–3 px wide.
Why it matters
T2 and T3 fail. Readers treat rank 53 against rank 60 as a real difference, which is exactly what the headlines do, when the data cannot separate them. Regional comparison (T7) means searching for scattered rows across three pages.
Improvement
Draw the rank range as a line on a rank axis, so the widening in the middle becomes a visible shape. Add an overview of the whole distribution. Use one continuous list with column headers that stay on screen, plus search and a region filter. Let each factor’s identity come from its column position, so the seven-colour legend is no longer needed.

    5. Design decisions

    D1. Unstack the bar into aligned factor columns on one shared scale

    fixes W1 T4 · T5
    What changed
    The seven stacked segments became six side-by-side columns plus a residual column, drawn in the same rows. Each column has its own zero line, and every column uses the same number of pixels per ladder point, so the width of a column shows how much that factor ranges across countries.
    Why
    Aligned position is judged far more accurately than unaligned length. With a shared scale the column widths make a point of their own: GDP spans 2.2 points and social support 1.7, while generosity spans only 0.3. This matches the report’s own decomposition (Statistical Appendix, Table 20), in which generosity accounts for 2% of the explained happiness, but the original chart never shows it.
    Improvement
    Answering “who gets the most out of social support?” now takes one click on the column title. Reading a country’s profile means scanning one row: Uzbekistan’s GDP bar points left (−0.12) while social support (+0.33), freedom (+0.20) and health (+0.16) point right.

    D2. Measure the factors from the average country, with Dystopia one click away

    fixes W3 T5 · audience
    What changed
    By default each bar shows the factor’s contribution minus its average across countries, drawn to the right in blue when it raises the predicted score and to the left in red when it lowers it. The toggle switches back to the report’s Dystopia benchmark. Only the zero line moves; the end of every bar stays where it is, which shows that the two views describe the same data.
    Why
    “Above or below a typical country” is how a general reader already thinks, and a two-sided bar shows a country’s weak points, which Dystopia hides by making every bar positive. Centring on the mean also takes away the dependence on the single lowest value, so the $89 Venezuelan figure no longer sets the zero point for every other country.
    Improvement
    China’s profile, for example, reads immediately: social support is below average (−0.24) and health is above (+0.29). In the original, both appear as positive segments of similar length.

    D3. Separate the score from its explanation

    fixes W2 T1 · T6
    What changed
    The life evaluation is now a dot with its 95% interval on its own axis, with the exact score printed beside it. The 1.16 constant is removed from every row and stated once, in the line above the table. The residual has its own column as a two-sided bar.
    Why
    The score is what the ranking is based on, so it should be the most prominent mark rather than the tip of a stack. The residual is a real and interesting quantity, but only once it is shown as a residual that can be negative, not mixed into a constant.
    Improvement
    No row can overshoot its score any more. Sorting the residual column answers T6 directly: Venezuela (hatched, because of the GDP value), Guatemala, Costa Rica, Mozambique and Mexico sit well above their prediction, while Hong Kong (−1.46), Sri Lanka and Botswana sit far below it.

    D4. Show uncertainty and distribution as shapes, not as bracketed text

    fixes W5 T2 · T3
    What changed
    A “plausible rank” column draws each country’s rank range as a line on a 1–147 axis. Hovering over a row shades every row whose rank falls inside that range, meaning the countries it could plausibly trade places with. Above the table, a beeswarm places all 147 scores on one axis, with brackets measuring the top 20 and the bottom 20.
    Why
    The report’s point about uncertainty is about a shape: ranges that are narrow at both ends of the table and wide in the middle. A shape should be drawn, not left for the reader to work out from 147 bracketed numbers.
    Improvement
    The rank lines are short at both ends of the table (a median of 5–6 places among the top and bottom fifteen) and about 20 places long through the middle, and that shape is now visible. Hovering over Uzbekistan highlights the 23 rows from rank 43 to 65, which is a direct answer to “is 53rd really different from 60th?”. The beeswarm shows at a glance that the top is crowded and the bottom is spread out.

    D5. One continuous, navigable view with two colours instead of seven

    fixes W4 · W5 T1 · T7
    What changed
    All 147 rows are in one list, with column headers that stay on screen while scrolling, a faint band every five rows to guide the eye across the wide layout, a search box, a region filter and pinning. Colour now has one job, direction (blue raises, red lowers), because the column already tells the reader which factor they are looking at. Missing cells say “no data”, and doubtful values are hatched and footnoted.
    Why
    Seven categorical hues that must be matched to a legend at the bottom of the page are a memory task, and page breaks cut the comparisons people actually want to make. Tajikistan’s five known factors are real information and should not be dropped because a sixth is missing.
    Improvement
    Choosing “Commonwealth of Independent States” puts Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan on one screen with their global ranks kept. Two colours pass the colour-vision deficiency check (worst-case ΔE 21.6).

    Original versus redesign

    TaskIn the originalIn the redesign
    T1 Look up Easy: rank and score are printed in every row. Just as easy. The rank and score are still printed, and search jumps straight to a country.
    T2 Is a gap real? Only by reading two bracketed ranges and comparing them in your head. Hover over a row: the countries it could trade places with are shaded.
    T3 Distribution Hidden. The bars form a smooth ramp across three pages. The beeswarm shows the crowded top and the long bottom tail, with the spreads measured.
    T4 One factor across countries Only GDP is readable; the other segments do not share a baseline. Every factor is an aligned column, and each can be sorted.
    T5 One country’s profile Seven colours to decode, every segment positive, and missing factors shown as blanks. Bars point left or right of average, and gaps say “no data”.
    T6 Outliers The residual is hidden inside “Dystopia + residual”, and negative residuals make the bar overshoot. Residual is its own two-sided column. Sort it to see both ends.
    T7 Within a region Not supported; the rows are scattered across three pages. The region filter keeps global ranks and shows the region in the beeswarm.

    Trade-offs and limitations

    • The parts no longer visibly add up to the whole. A stacked bar shows that the pieces sum to the score just by its length. Here that sum is written as an equation above the table. I think this is the right trade, because the stacked sum was the thing that broke for Hong Kong and Sri Lanka, but it does ask more of the reader.
    • The default view differs from the report’s numbers. A reader checking a value against the PDF has to switch to Dystopia mode first. The table view always shows the report’s own values.
    • The shared scale makes some columns thin. The generosity bars span only about 12 px. That is honest, because generosity really does vary very little, but comparing two similar countries on it depends on sorting or the tooltip rather than on bar length alone.
    • It is built for a screen. The layout is about 1,150 px wide and scales down on phones. Hover, pinning and sorting do not survive printing, and a print version would need small multiples by region.
    • The data has limits the chart cannot fix. The “explained by” values are regression contributions, not causal effects, even when the bars are labelled “raises” and “lowers”. Rank ranges were read from the PDF, and regions come from the 2021 classification. The view also covers one period only, 2023–2025, so change over time is not shown.
