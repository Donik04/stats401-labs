Dataset description
Tweets carrying the #covid19 hashtag, collected daily between 24 July and 30 August 2020, with the raw tweet text and the posting account's metadata. No sentiment labels ship with the data — sentiment here is computed, not read off the file.
Data source
Gabriel Preda, COVID19 Tweets (179,108 tweets). Downloaded and sampled by fetch_tweets.py, then cleaned and scored by clean_tweets.py.
Records
1,040 tweets sampled from the source, 1,035 surviving cleaning, each carrying a sentiment estimate.
Sentiment
Estimated with cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest, a RoBERTa Transformer fine-tuned on social-media text. These are model estimates, not ground-truth labels.

Does it matter who is doing the typing?

Every tweet records the client it was posted from. Most come from a phone or from the Twitter website — someone sat down and wrote them. A smaller share arrives through newsroom and marketing tooling: TweetDeck, or scheduling suites like Hootsuite and Buffer, where a tweet is drafted, approved and queued rather than fired off. If an organisation's tweets go through a different process than a person's, that process might show up in what the tweets sound like.

Analysis

The data is 1,040 tweets carrying #covid19, sampled 40 per day across the 26 collection days of a 179,108-tweet archive gathered in July and August 2020. Cleaning cost 5 rows: 4 near-duplicate campaign templates reposted with fresh tracking links, and 1 non-English tweet, leaving 1,035 scored tweets. The larger job was source, where 71 client strings — one spelled with a Greek omicron, others with trailing spaces — collapse onto seven posting routes; is_retweet was constant and dropped. No sentiment ships with the data, so every tweet was scored with RoBERTa, and the chart plots the resulting shares diverging from a common centre.

The negative share falls steadily as the route gets more institutional: 39% of tweets typed on a phone, 32% from the Twitter website, 21% from TweetDeck, 14% from a scheduling suite. Mean score follows it from −0.20 to +0.01. The other end of the bar barely moves — positive runs 9–14% on all four routes. Organisational tweets are not cheerier than personal ones; they trade anger for neutrality, which is what drafted-and-queued copy sounds like. With 53 tweets behind TweetDeck against 445 behind phones, though, only the two ends of that ordering are firm.

Optional extension — where the model is unsure

The model returns a probability for each of the three classes, and the label is simply the largest of the three. That number is worth looking at on its own. A tweet called negative at 0.99 and one called negative at 0.41 — the two ends of the range here — are not the same claim, but they arrive in the dataset looking identical.

How sure the model was of the label it picked, across every scored tweet. The lowest possible value is about 0.33, where all three classes are tied; anything under 0.50 means the winning class did not even carry half the probability.

The tweets it found hardest

These are the tweets where the top two classes came closest to a tie — where the label is near enough to a coin flip that it should not be trusted on its own. Reading them shows what the model struggles with.

How the data was cleaned

fetch_tweets.py downloads the full 69 MB source file and writes 40 tweets from each of the 26 collection days to lab4_raw_tweets.csv, completely unmodified. Sampling by day rather than at random keeps the whole five-week window visible instead of letting one busy week crowd out the rest, and 1,040 rows leaves a margin over the 1,000 the assignment asks for once cleaning has taken its cut.

clean_tweets.py then does the work, printing a report of every decision as it goes. Tweets with no text are dropped, because they cannot be scored. Missing locations and hashtags are left missing rather than filled with zeros: "this account stated no location" is a fact about the account, and replacing it would invent one. Exact duplicates turn out to be rare in this sample, but the same campaign text is posted repeatedly with a different tracking link on the end, so tweets are also compared with links, case and punctuation stripped out. That catches the near-copies which would otherwise let a single petition template dominate a sentiment total. is_retweet is False on every row, so it separates nothing and is dropped.

The source column is the interesting one. It holds 71 distinct client strings, including one written with a Greek omicron in place of a Latin O, several carrying trailing spaces or stray full stops, and a long tail of one-off corporate apps. Lookalike letters are folded onto their Latin equivalents, whitespace and punctuation are normalised, and the result is collapsed onto seven posting routes — exact matching for the official clients, keyword rules for everything else. user_location is free text with thousands of spellings; a keyword pass recovers a country where it can and leaves the rest missing rather than guessing.

The sentiment branch deliberately preserves what the term branch throws away. TF-IDF wants lemmatised content words, so URLs, mentions, numbers, stop words and punctuation all go. RoBERTa wants the sentence, so capitalisation, emoji, punctuation and negation stay, and only usernames and links are replaced with the placeholders the model was trained on.

On provenance. The upstream repository publishes the collection openly but states no licence, and Twitter's own terms restrict redistributing tweet content, so nothing here should be treated as cleared for reuse. The sampled raw file is kept in this repository because the assignment asks for the input as well as the output, and because a cleaning script cannot be checked against data nobody can see. Anyone who would rather not rely on that copy can rebuild it exactly: fetch_tweets.py fetches the original file and re-derives the same sample from a fixed seed.

One caveat the data forces. The collector capped itself at roughly 7,500 tweets a day and missed several days entirely, so the number of tweets on a given day reflects the collection process rather than how much the world was tweeting. Daily counts are therefore not interpretable here, though daily mean sentiment still is — which is why the chart above compares shares rather than volumes.
